Valley Living and Water

Elevation is destiny in plumbing. Valley-floor homes sit at the bottom of the local watershed, which means three things. First, the water table rises dramatically in the wet season — basements and crawlspaces that were dry in August can take on water in November. Second, stormwater systems work hardest here; when ditches, culverts, and storm drains can't keep up, the overflow looks for the lowest point, which might be your driveway or garage. Third, the sanitary sewer system itself can surcharge during extreme events — when the system is overwhelmed, sewage can push backward through your lateral and into your home through the lowest drain. None of this is hypothetical; every few years, a big atmospheric river reminds the valley how water works. The homeowners who fare best aren't the lucky ones — they're the prepared ones, with equipment in place and a plan taped inside the utility closet door.

Your Flood-Season Prep Checklist

Do this every September, before the rains. Test your sump pump: pour a bucket of water into the pit and confirm it cycles on, pumps down, and shuts off. Check the battery backup — press the test button, verify the charge, and replace batteries older than three years. Walk your discharge line outside and confirm it's clear, unfrozen, and directing water well away from the foundation (a discharge that dumps next to the house just recycles the problem). Inspect your backwater valve if you have one — we'll explain below why valley homes should. Clean gutters and confirm downspouts discharge away from the house, not into a pipe that ends at the foundation. Check grading: soil should slope away from your home for at least six feet. Locate your main water shutoff and your sewer cleanout now, not during an emergency, and make sure every adult in the house knows where they are. This checklist takes an afternoon. The flood it prevents takes weeks to recover from.

Sump Pumps and Backwater Valves Explained

These are the two devices that define valley-floor plumbing defense, and they do different jobs. A sump pump removes groundwater: it sits in a pit at your home's lowest point, collects water seeping in from the surrounding soil, and pumps it outside and away. Every valley home with a basement or low crawlspace should have one — plus a battery backup, because the storms that cause flooding also cause power outages, and a pump without power is a decoration. A backwater valve protects your sanitary line: it's a one-way flap installed on your sewer lateral that lets wastewater flow out but slams shut if sewage tries to flow backward into your home. During system surcharges, it's the difference between a scare and a biohazard cleanup. When the Storm Hits: Emergency Steps

Preparation covers most scenarios, but when water is actively coming in, think clearly and act in order. First, electricity and water don't mix: if water is approaching outlets, your electrical panel, or the furnace, stay out of the water and call for help — no basement is worth an electrocution risk. Move valuables, documents, and electronics to higher ground if you can do so safely. If sewage is backing up through drains, stop using water immediately — every flush and every faucet makes it worse — and keep people and pets away from the backup. Find your main water shutoff if a pipe has burst or a fixture is running wild. Then call us: our emergency line is answered 24/7, and valley flooding is exactly the kind of call we prioritize.
